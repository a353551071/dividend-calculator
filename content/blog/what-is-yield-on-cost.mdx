<p><strong>Yield on cost (YOC) is the annual dividend a stock pays you divided by the price you originally paid for it.</strong> Buy a stock at $50 that now pays $3 a year in dividends, and your yield on cost is 6% — even if the share price has doubled since. Where the regular dividend yield tracks today's price, yield on cost tracks your own cost basis, which is why long-term dividend investors watch it climb year after year.</p>

<h2>Yield on cost formula</h2>

<p><strong>Yield on cost = Annual dividend per share ÷ Average cost per share</strong></p>

<p>Worked example: you bought 100 shares at $40 each, a $4,000 position. The company has raised its dividend from $1.60 to $2.40 over the years. Your yield on cost is $2.40 ÷ $40 = <strong>6%</strong>, while a new buyer at $80 sees only a 3% current yield. Same dividend, two very different realities — that gap is the entire appeal of buy-and-hold dividend investing.</p>

<p>To see how reinvested dividends accelerate this, run your numbers through the <a href="/drip-calculator">DRIP calculator</a>; reinvestment buys more shares, which pay more dividends, which raises your effective yield on cost further.</p>

<h2>Why yield on cost rises every year you hold</h2>

<p>The denominator never changes. Your cost is locked in on the day you buy, but dividend-growth companies raise the numerator annually. A company growing its dividend 7% a year doubles it in about a decade. Hold quality dividend growers long enough and the yield on cost can exceed 100% of the original investment — the stock pays you more each year than you paid for it in total, while you still own the shares.</p>

<p>This mechanic is why yield on cost is the natural companion to a <a href="/how-dividend-reinvestment-works-drip">dividend reinvestment plan</a>: reinvested dividends compound the position, and each raise compounds on top of the larger holding.</p>

<h2>The trap: yield on cost is a rearview mirror</h2>

<p>The metric has honest critics. Your cost basis is irrelevant to anyone else — the market prices the stock on its future, not your past. A stock with a 9% yield on cost and deteriorating fundamentals is not automatically a hold; the alternative today is selling and redeploying at the current yield elsewhere. Use yield on cost to <em>measure</em> what patient ownership has earned you, not to <em>justify</em> holding a broken story. Pair it with the payout ratio: a rising yield on cost only matters if the dividend is safe.</p>

<h2>How to grow yield on cost faster</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Buy quality at a reasonable price.</strong> The lower the entry cost, the higher every future dividend reads as yield.</li>
  <li><strong>Favor dividend growers over high current yielders.</strong> Growth compounds the numerator; a frozen payout freezes your YOC.</li>
  <li><strong>Reinvest dividends</strong> during flat and down markets, when reinvestment prices are lowest — the shares bought cheapest raise your average yield on cost the most.</li>
  <li><strong>Check affordability before buying:</strong> a dividend growing 8% a year from an 85% payout ratio is a promise that math will eventually break. Start with the <a href="/dividend-payout-ratio-calculator">payout ratio</a>, then project the income with the <a href="/dividend-growth-calculator">dividend growth calculator</a>.</li>
</ul>
