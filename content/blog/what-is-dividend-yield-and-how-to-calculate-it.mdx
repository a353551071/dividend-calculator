<p><strong>Dividend yield</strong> measures how much annual cash income a stock or ETF pays back relative to its current share price, expressed as a percentage. Along with payout safety and dividend growth, it is the core valuation metric income investors use to compare cash flow across equities.</p>

<h2>The dividend yield formula</h2>

<p><strong>Dividend Yield = (Annual Dividend per Share &divide; Current Share Price) &times; 100</strong></p>

<p>Suppose a company distributes <strong>$0.75 per share every quarter</strong> and currently trades at <strong>$75.00 per share</strong>:</p>

<ol>
  <li>Annualize the dividend: $0.75 &times; 4 quarters = <strong>$3.00 per year</strong>.</li>
  <li>Divide by current price: $3.00 &divide; $75.00 = <strong>0.04</strong>.</li>
  <li>Convert to percentage: 0.04 &times; 100 = <strong>4.00% dividend yield</strong>.</li>
</ol>

<p>For <strong>monthly dividend stocks or ETFs</strong> (such as Realty Income, JEPI, or QQQI), multiply the monthly payout by 12 instead of 4. Note that option-income ETFs pay variable monthly distributions, so annualizing a single unusually large month can overstate sustainable forward yield.</p>

<h2>Trailing (TTM) yield vs. forward yield</h2>

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Metric</th><th>How It Is Calculated</th><th>When to Use It</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td><strong>Trailing Twelve Months (TTM)</strong></td><td>Sum of the last 4 quarterly (or 12 monthly) declared payouts &divide; current share price</td><td>Reliable baseline for irregular, special, or variable option-income ETFs</td></tr>
    <tr><td><strong>Forward Dividend Yield</strong></td><td>Most recent regular dividend &times; payout frequency &divide; current share price</td><td>More accurate right after a company announces a dividend hike or cut</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<h2>Why dividend yield changes without a dividend change</h2>

<p>Yield moves inversely to share price every trading day. If a stock paying $4.00/year drops from $100 to $80 (-20%), its yield jumps mechanically from <strong>4.0% to 5.0%</strong> even though the company did not raise its payout. Conversely, when a strong stock rallies +25%, its headline current yield drops from 4.0% to 3.2% for new buyers — while earlier shareholders still earn their original <a href="/blog/what-is-yield-on-cost">yield on cost</a>.</p>

<h2>Why ultra-high yields can be a value trap</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Healthy income profile:</strong> A 3%–5% yield paired with a conservative payout ratio (&lt;60% of earnings or free cash flow) usually reflects durable cash generation.</li>
  <li><strong>Warning sign (&gt;8%–10% outside REITs/BDCs):</strong> An unusually elevated yield compared with sector peers often means the market expects an upcoming dividend cut — and when a company cuts its dividend, both your income and share price drop together.</li>
</ul>
