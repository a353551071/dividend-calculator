<p><strong>The ex-dividend date is the first day a stock trades without its next dividend attached.</strong> Buy before it and you receive the upcoming payment; buy on it or after and the seller keeps it. Of the four dates in every dividend cycle, this is the one that decides who gets paid, which is why income investors mark it on the calendar before anything else.</p>

<h2>The four dates of every dividend</h2>

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Date</th><th>What happens</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td><strong>Declaration date</strong></td><td>The company announces the amount and the coming dates</td></tr>
    <tr><td><strong>Ex-dividend date</strong></td><td>The cutoff: buy before this day to qualify for the dividend</td></tr>
    <tr><td><strong>Record date</strong></td><td>The company records who the shareholders are (usually one business day after the ex-date under T+1 settlement)</td></tr>
    <tr><td><strong>Payment date</strong></td><td>The cash actually lands in your account</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<p>Because US stocks settle the next business day, buying on the day before the ex-date is the last chance for your trade to settle by the record date. That is why the simple rule exists: <strong>own the stock before the ex-dividend date, and the payment is yours.</strong></p>

<h2>What the ex-date does to the share price</h2>

<p>On the morning of the ex-dividend date, exchanges reduce the share price by roughly the dividend amount. A $100 stock paying a $1 dividend opens near $99 on the ex-date. Nothing was lost — the dollar moved from the share price into your upcoming payment — but it explains why dividend-heavy portfolios show small dips on schedule. Funds that hold these stocks (or track a <a href="/dividend-calendar">calendar of ex-dividend dates</a>) absorb these micro-movements constantly.</p>

<h2>The dividend-capture trap</h2>

<p>A classic idea: buy the day before the ex-date, collect the dividend, sell immediately. In practice the price adjustment eats most of the gain, short-term-held dividends are usually taxed as ordinary income, and spreads plus fees finish the rest. Dividends reward ownership, not parking. The investors who actually collect meaningful income hold through the cycle and reinvest — see how that compounds with the <a href="/dividend-reinvestment-calculator">dividend reinvestment calculator</a>.</p>

<h2>Ex-dividend dates for popular dividend ETFs</h2>

<p>Monthly payers make the rhythm easy to see. <strong>QQQI</strong> and <strong>JEPI</strong> go ex-dividend near the start of each month and pay a few business days later, so a monthly income stream requires simply holding them. <strong>SCHD</strong> pays quarterly, with ex-dates typically in mid-March, June, September, and December. Each cycle's exact dates shift, so check the live schedules in the <a href="/schd-dividend-calculator">SCHD dividend calendar</a>, <a href="/qqqi-dividend-calculator">QQQI dividend calendar</a>, and our aggregated <a href="/dividend-calendar">dividend calendar hub</a> before planning cash flow around a payment.</p>

<h2>Planning income around ex-dates</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Ladder monthly payers</strong> (JEPI, QQQI, Realty Income) so a payment lands every week or two rather than once a quarter.</li>
  <li><strong>Watch quarterly ex-dates for SCHD-style funds</strong> if you use dividends to cover predictable expenses like taxes or insurance premiums.</li>
  <li><strong>Do not buy a fund right before its ex-date just for the payout</strong> — you are withdrawing your own money from the price. Let the schedule inform holding, not chasing.</li>
</ul>
