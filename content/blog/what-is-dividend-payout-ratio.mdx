<p><strong>The dividend payout ratio is the percentage of a company's earnings that it pays out to shareholders as dividends.</strong> It answers one question: can this company afford its dividend? A ratio of 40% means 40 cents of every dollar of profit goes to dividends and the rest is reinvested or kept as a cushion. The ratio is one of the first numbers income investors check, because an unsustainable payout is the most common early warning before a dividend cut.</p>

<h2>Dividend payout ratio formula</h2>

<p>The formula is simple:</p>

<p><strong>Payout ratio = Dividends per share ÷ Earnings per share (EPS)</strong></p>

<p>Example: a company earns $4.00 per share and pays a $1.60 annual dividend. The payout ratio is $1.60 ÷ $4.00 = <strong>40%</strong>. You can compute the same figure with totals: total dividends paid ÷ net income.</p>

<p>A quick way to test any stock's payout is the <a href="/dividend-payout-ratio-calculator">dividend payout ratio calculator</a>, which also shows how the ratio shifts as dividends grow.</p>

<h2>How to read the number</h2>

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Payout ratio</th><th>Typical reading</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Under 30%</td><td>Very safe, lots of room to raise the dividend; common for dividend-growth companies</td></tr>
    <tr><td>30% to 60%</td><td>Healthy balance for mature companies; the sweet spot for most income investors</td></tr>
    <tr><td>60% to 80%</td><td>Still payable, but sensitive to an earnings dip</td></tr>
    <tr><td>80% to 100%</td><td>Warning zone; little margin for error</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Above 100%</td><td>Paying out more than it earns; usually unsustainable outside REITs</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<h2>Warning signs that a payout is unsustainable</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>The ratio stays above 100% for several quarters.</strong> One bad quarter is noise; a pattern means the dividend is being funded by debt or reserves.</li>
  <li><strong>Earnings fall but the dividend keeps rising.</strong> The gap has to close eventually, either through recovery or a cut.</li>
  <li><strong>The yield is far above sector peers.</strong> An unusually high <a href="/what-is-dividend-yield-and-how-to-calculate-it">dividend yield</a> often signals the market expects a cut, not a bargain.</li>
  <li><strong>Rising debt alongside steady dividends.</strong> Borrowing to pay dividends shows up in the balance sheet before it shows up in the press release.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Payout ratio versus retention ratio</h2>

<p>The two numbers are mirror images. The retention ratio is what remains after dividends: 1 − payout ratio. A 40% payout means a 60% retention, which is the capital the company keeps for growth, buybacks, or debt reduction. Faster-growing companies tend toward high retention; mature cash machines can afford high payout.</p>

<h2>Special cases worth knowing</h2>

<p><strong>REITs and MLPs</strong> are required to pay out most of their income, so payout ratios above 90% are structural, not a red flag. Analysts use FFO or free cash flow payout for these names instead. <strong>Covered-call ETFs</strong> such as JEPI or QQQI distribute option income rather than plain earnings, so a traditional payout ratio barely applies; their distributions are better judged against net asset value and option premium trends. <strong>Cyclicals</strong> (miners, energy) swing between safe and extreme ratios as commodity prices move, so a single year's ratio misleads; use averages across a cycle.</p>

<h2>How investors actually use it</h2>

<p>In practice the payout ratio works as a screen, not a verdict. Income investors often require a ratio under 60% for regular companies and accept high ratios only in REITs. Dividend-growth investors prefer low ratios because each raise is easy to afford. And when a stock's <a href="/how-dividend-reinvestment-works-drip">dividend reinvestment plan</a> compounds over decades, a safe payout today matters more than a headline yield, because a cut breaks the whole compounding chain.</p>
